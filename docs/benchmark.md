# How good is the gate?

Tripwire blocks a merge on the strength of a statistical verdict, so the verdict itself
has to be tested. This page says how that was done and what came out. Every number is
generated by `python experiments/proof.py`; the full tables are in
[experiments/reports/proof.md](../experiments/reports/proof.md).

## The idea

Make changes whose real effect is known, then see what the gate says about each when it
only gets to look at a sample.

1. **A zoo of deliberate changes.** `experiments/zoo.py` writes 28 variants of the
   Banking77 setup, each an ordinary suite: edits meant to hurt (few-shot examples removed
   or mislabelled, intents dropped from the label list, the customer's message cut short,
   the temperature raised, the answer limit cut, a contradictory instruction, a prompt too
   long for its context window), edits meant to change nothing (whitespace, synonyms,
   reordered instructions, examples and labels, renamed fields, a polite sentence, another
   API for the same model, a smaller context window that still fits), and two other models.
2. **The truth from a large run.** Every variant answers all 1,500 cases of the reference
   split once. Its score minus the baseline's on those cases is its real effect.
3. **Sorted by what it did, not by what it was meant to do.** A variant fell *beyond the
   margin* (more than 3 points), *inside the margin*, or showed *no drop*. Two more
   outcomes get their own name: *identical scores*, and *invalid*, where so many answers
   could not be scored that the gate refuses to give a verdict at all.
4. **The gate, replayed.** For each variant, a thousand gates: draw 700 cases at random,
   take the first 250 as the first stage, and decide exactly as the real gate does, with
   the same interval and the same verdict rule. Three designs are counted on the same
   draws: one look at 250 cases, one look at 700, and the staged gate that looks at 250
   with half the error budget and at all 700 only if those cannot decide.
5. **A plain threshold for comparison.** On the same draws: block when accuracy drops at
   all, or when it drops by more than the margin, with no interval.

### Two choices that matter

**Cases are drawn with replacement.** Drawing subsets of 700 from a pool of 1,500 looks
natural and is wrong here. Almost half the pool is in every subset, so subsets resemble
the whole, the spread between them shrinks by `sqrt(1 - 700/1500) = 0.73`, and the gate
looks both more powerful and more cautious than it is. Drawn with replacement, every draw
is an independent sample from a population in which the variant's effect is exactly the
one measured on all 1,500 cases. The truth the gate is checked against is then known
without error.

**False alarms are measured on changes with the direction taken out.** A harmless
variant's effect on 1,500 cases is never exactly zero: one that happened to land at −0.006
really is a small drop in that population, and a gate that flags it has not made an
error. So the false-alarm rate is not read off the harmless-looking variants. Instead
every variant is replayed a second time with the two sides of each drawn case swapped at
random. Answers then change exactly as often as they really did, but in neither direction
more than the other: a change of that size with no effect at all. Any `REGRESSED` or
`IMPROVED` verdict there is a false alarm, and it is measured at each variant's own rate
of change, from under 2% of answers to more than 40%.

## Results

All of it is in [proof.md](../experiments/reports/proof.md). The short version, for
Banking77 on `gemma3:4b` (baseline 0.604 on 1,500 cases, margin 3 points, alpha 5%):

![How often the gate says REGRESSED, by the true size of the change](../experiments/reports/dose_response.svg)

| | 250 cases | 700 cases | Staged 250 → 700 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drop beyond the margin (7 variants, 3.4 to 39.6 points): called `REGRESSED` | 89% | 99% | 98% |
| The same: let through as `PASS` or `IMPROVED` | 1% (worst 4%) | 0% (worst 3%) | 1% (worst 3%) |
| No effect (27 variants, sides swapped): called `REGRESSED` | 4% (worst 6%) | 3% (worst 6%) | 4% (worst 5%) |
| No drop (10 variants): passed | 78% | 95% | 93% |
| Cases run per decision | 250 | 700 | 314 to 435 on average |

**Real regressions are caught.** Every drop of 5.8 points or more was called `REGRESSED`
in 100% of gates at 700 cases, to the nearest percent, and in at least 83% at 250. The one variant just
past the margin, all 16 examples mislabelled at −3.4 points, was caught 92% of the time at
700 cases and 57% at 250. A drop beyond the margin was let through in at most 4% of gates.

**The false-alarm rate is where alpha says it should be.** With the direction taken out,
`REGRESSED` came back in 3 to 4% of gates on average against a nominal 5%. The worst
single variant reached 6%, which is within what a thousand draws can tell from 5%.

**Small drops are a grey zone by design.** Ten variants fell by less than the margin.
`REGRESSED` means "confidently worse", not "worse than the margin", so a real 2.7-point
drop (the message cut to 60 characters) is called `REGRESSED` in 88% of gates at 700
cases, and a 0.3-point one almost never.

**Stopping early has a cost.** For changes that move few answers (a rerun, a new salt, a
halved context window: about 2% of scores), one look at 250 cases calls a drop of 0.2 to
0.6 points `REGRESSED` in 7 to 14% of gates, where one look at 700 says so in 0 to 2%. The
staged gate inherits this (7 to 15%), because a `REGRESSED` first look is final. That is
not a breach of alpha, since the drop is real in that population, but it is what the 38 to
55% saving in cases is paid with.

**A plain threshold fails in both directions.** "Block on any drop" blocks 23 to 26% of
the changes that did nothing and 67 to 77% of the small ones. "Block on a drop over the
margin" lets through 6 to 8% of the regressions beyond it, where Tripwire lets through 0
to 1%.

**What the edits really did** was not what they were meant to do, which is why they are
sorted by measurement:

- Shuffling the label list, meant to be harmless, *raised* accuracy by 3.1 points, as much
  as 48 extra examples did (+3.4).
- Raising the temperature to 1.5 changed 0.5% of scores. The answers are a few tokens
  long and the model is sure of them.
- Halving the examples, and telling the model to answer in a full sentence, did nothing
  measurable.
- Cutting the answer limit to 4 tokens left 98.6% of answers cut off. Every gate returned
  `INVALID`, not `REGRESSED`: the plumbing broke, and the gate says so.
- The same model through the OpenAI-compatible endpoint gave identical scores.
- `qwen2.5:7b` is not better than `gemma3:4b` here (+0.7 points, interval [−1.2, +2.8]),
  so the swap exercises `PASS`, not `IMPROVED`. With the real tool the two differ by
  slice (exchange questions +15 points for `qwen2.5:7b`, cash and payments −7.5) and by
  output length (0.65 of the tokens); swapping back trips both the slice flag and the
  output-token guardrail. `gemma3:1b` scores 0.208 and is blocked.

**Slices.** Among changes that did not fall beyond the margin, a slice flag came up in 12%
of gates and blocked a change the overall verdict would have passed in 4%. Most of those
are real: with 48 more examples the total rose 3.4 points while identity questions fell
11.4, and a slice was flagged in 38% of gates.

**A second repetition** of three variants with new seeds moved their effects by at most
0.7 points and changed no conclusion.

**Judged metrics** (policy QA, 240 cases, gates of 120 and 240). Removing the abstain
instruction (−12.9 points on `judge.correct`) was caught in every gate. The synonym
rewording, meant to be harmless, cost 2.1 points of correctness and 4.6 of groundedness.
False alarms averaged 4 to 6%; the worst were 8%, on a 120-case look and on the staged gate
that starts with one, where there are very few changed answers to resample. With one variant beyond the margin
this part shows the method carries over, and little more.

## What an overflowing prompt does

One variant exists because of something measured along the way. Ollama 0.35.1 does two
things with the context window that are easy to miss:

- **A window below 2,048 tokens is raised to 2,048.** Asking for `num_ctx` 512 or 1,024
  loads the model with 2,048. A prompt that fits in 2,048 therefore cannot be made to
  overflow by shrinking the setting.
- **A prompt longer than the window is cut to about half of it, without any error.**
  Given 2,308 tokens for a 2,048-token window, the server kept 1,027: the first 5 and the
  last 1,022. The reply is an ordinary reply, and the reported prompt length is the
  shortened one, so nothing in the response says that anything was dropped.

The pair of variants built on this: the baseline prompt with 48 more few-shot examples,
once with the 4,096-token window it fits in and once with a 2,048-token window it does
not. In the second, the instruction and the whole list of 77 labels are gone before the
model reads anything, and what is left is a tail of examples. With room, the extra examples help:
0.638, up 3.4 points. Without it, 0.342, down 26.4: the reported prompt length is 1,027
tokens against 2,306, and 79% of answers differ from the baseline's. At least 99.6% of the
thousand gates called it `REGRESSED`, at 250 cases and at 700.

This is why a run checks the longest prompt against the window before it starts, and why
that check now covers the judge's prompts too.

## Limits

- **It is a benchmark of the gate's statistics, on changes its author chose.** The zoo,
  the gate and the analysis come from one person. The results say how the gate behaves on
  these seeded changes to one prompt for one 4B model; they are not evidence about every
  regression a team will meet.
- **One stored answer per case.** A draw varies which cases the gate sees, not what the
  model said. That is the randomness a real gate has, since its samples are seeded and a
  fixed set of cases always gives the same answers, but it leaves out what a different
  seed would do. The second-repetition table is the check on this for a few variants.
- **The population is 1,500 cases.** Within the replay the truth is exact. How well those
  cases pin down each variant's effect on Banking77 at large is the interval in the first
  table, about ±1.5 points for most variants.
- **The verdict on the primary metric only.** The replay does not model the guardrails
  (output length, latency, truncation rate), which can also block. Slice flags are
  measured separately.
- **The simulated gate resamples 2,000 times per interval** where the real gate uses
  10,000. The intervals are the same construction.
- **Banking77 is public and its labels are noisy.** Noise caps the accuracy any prompt can
  reach; it does not change differences between two prompts scored on the same cases.

## Regenerating it

```bash
python experiments/zoo.py                                   # the suites, prompts and targets
tripwire queue -c experiments/zoo.toml --match "zoo-*"      # about 5 hours on the reference GPU
tripwire queue -c experiments/zoo.toml --match "swap-*"
tripwire queue -c experiments/zoo.toml --match "qa-*"
python experiments/proof.py --extract                       # scores.json from the database
python experiments/proof.py                                 # the report and the figure
```

The last step needs no model and no database: `experiments/reports/scores.json` holds one
pass or fail per variant and case, so the tables can be regenerated from a clone.
